Motivation

A model can get the answer right without asking follow-up questions.

When a user's request is underspecified, a capable assistant should recognize that the current context is insufficient, identify what information is missing, ask targeted questions, integrate the responses, and commit only once the answer becomes determined. This capability is central to interactive assistants, yet remains difficult to evaluate directly and systematically.

Existing multi-turn benchmarks often emphasize final task success, dialogue quality, or user-facing outcomes, without directly specifying what information should be requested or whether the model stopped only after the target became identifiable. As a result, evaluation can be confounded by model priors and answer-generation ability — final accuracy may overstate information-seeking ability more than it reveals it.

In MediQ, a 30B model gets 60–70% of cases right from the initial, incomplete prompt — without asking any follow-up questions.

Structured formulations can reduce this ambiguity. QuestBench formalizes underspecified reasoning as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem and provides the ground-truth missing variable a model should query — but it only covers the special case where a single missing variable is sufficient. Multi-turn information seeking is fundamentally different: multiple variables may be jointly necessary, and the model must decide not just what to ask, but in what order and when to stop. Capturing this requires representing joint sufficiency and tracking how each queried value reshapes the feasible space over successive turns — dimensions that single-turn query selection or final-answer accuracy alone cannot see.

Overview

A controlled framework for multi-turn information seeking.

We formalize multi-turn information seeking as solving a k-underspecified constraint satisfaction problem, where k denotes the number of variables jointly required to determine the target. Each problem specifies constraints, observations, a target variable, and minimal sufficient query sets; the model queries in multiple turns to gather missing information and identify a hidden target.

Using this formulation, we study LLMs along three axes — what they ask, when they ask it, and how the acquired information affects the final answer — separating information-seeking behavior from forward reasoning to reveal model differences that final-answer accuracy alone obscures.

Datasets

MT-InfoSeek spans mathematical, biological, clinical, and open-ended settings.

The benchmark contains 5,251 underspecified problems and 9,006 induced task instances across mathematical, logical, biological, clinical, and open-ended settings.

Logic-Q-MT Logical Reasoning

Rule-based logical reasoning environments where models recursively identify and query the missing facts needed to prove or disprove a target proposition.

GSME-Q-MT Math

Grade-school math word problems with human-annotated missing conditions required to compute an answer, extended to multi-turn by masking multiple quantities at once.

GSME-Q-MT-Ext Math

A more diagnostic math variant generated from DAG-structured arithmetic programs, with deeper dependency chains, larger variable sets, and rigorously validated k-MSSs.

GeneReg-MT Biological

Boolean gene regulatory networks where variables are gene-expression states and the target is a steady state or a specific marker-gene value, testing navigation of cyclic dependencies.

ClinGuide-MT Clinical

Diagnostic decision trees derived from clinical guidelines, whose root-to-leaf pathways encode conditional dependencies among symptoms, tests, and findings.

20Q Open-Ended

The classic 20 Questions game as an open-domain setting: each natural-language yes/no question dynamically partitions a finite candidate set instead of querying a predefined variable.

Problem setup

A problem is a tuple of variables, domains, constraints, an observed partial assignment, and a target variable. A set of unknown variables is sufficient if fixing their values always determines the target; the smallest such set is the minimal k-sufficient set (k-MSS), whose size k defines the degree of underspecification. Each problem induces a family of task instances that share the same constraints and observations but differ in the hidden target value.

Takeaway 1 (RQ1)

LLMs recognize when context is insufficient, but underestimate how much additional information is required. Models rarely classify underspecified problems as fully specified, but their accuracy in predicting k drops as k increases.

Takeaway 2 (RQ1)

Knowing that information is missing does not mean knowing what information is needed. Providing the true k improves performance only modestly; exact-set accuracy remains low for k >= 2.

MSS exact-set accuracy with k not provided or provided.

Takeaway 3 (RQ1)

Sequential interaction improves information acquisition beyond total query count alone. In Logic-Q-MT and GeneReg-MT, final sufficiency generally increases as more turns are allowed.

Final sufficiency under different turn and per-turn query budgets.

Takeaway 4 (RQ1)

In highly underspecified problems, success depends less on asking the perfect first question and more on sustaining effective information acquisition across turns. Early-turn correctness correlates with success for small k, while late-turn correctness and turn count matter more as k grows.

Correlation between behavioral features and final sufficiency in Logic-Q-MT.

Takeaway 5 (RQ1)

An incorrect first question is often recoverable if the model continues searching for target-relevant information. In Logic-Q-MT, first-turn miss plus second-turn hit achieves final sufficiency close to first-turn-hit dialogues.

Final sufficiency for first-turn hit versus first-turn miss followed by second-turn hit.

Takeaway 6 (RQ2)

In order-dependent settings, asking the right questions is not enough if they are asked in the wrong order. Clinical diagnostic pathways impose conditional dependencies, and violating them reduces final accuracy.

ClinGuide-MT final accuracy stratified by MSS coverage and MSS ordering.

Takeaway 7 (RQ3)

Asking informative questions is necessary but not sufficient for successful multi-turn reasoning. Models with similar question quality can differ substantially in final accuracy, indicating that success also depends on evidence integration.

Takeaway 8 (RQ3)

LLMs are better at coarse-grained narrowing than fine-grained disambiguation. Remaining entropy decreases across turns, but curves often flatten once the candidate space has already been narrowed.

Takeaway 9 (RQ3)

Ambiguous questions are not always failures. High pass-support mass does not consistently imply lower final accuracy, because pass feedback can still provide partial evidence.

Conclusion

Multi-turn information seeking is a distinct capability.

Across logical, mathematical, biological, clinical, and open-ended tasks, current LLMs show a consistent gap: they often detect underspecification, but underestimate its degree, ask incomplete or less target-relevant questions, and stop before the target is uniquely determined.

These findings show that interactive competence cannot be assessed by final-answer accuracy alone. Models should also be evaluated by whether they know when context is insufficient, what information is needed, and how to obtain it over sustained interaction — separating the ability to answer from the ability to seek the information needed to answer.
